Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Glass Plates

Reassembling Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii's century-old red, green, and blue exposures into single color images — first by brute-force search, then with an image pyramid to make it fast enough for full-resolution scans.

Catherine Zhang

Each glass plate negative is really three photographs of the same scene, stacked vertically, taken through red, green, and blue filters. Stack the three channels directly and you get a ghostly, misaligned mess — the camera (or the photographer) moved slightly between exposures. The job of this project is to find, for each channel, the (x, y) shift that brings it back into register with the others.

The core idea is simple: try a shift, score how well it lines up, keep the best one. The interesting problems all show up once you ask how to search efficiently, how to score a match, and what to do when the naive approach breaks down on full-resolution scans.

Single-scale alignment

On the low-resolution JPEGs — cathedral, monastery, tobolsk

For the small JPEGs, brute force is fast enough to just work. I split each image into thirds to get the B, G, and R channels, then aligned G and R to B by searching every integer shift in a [−15, 15] window on both axes and scoring each candidate.

Two details mattered more than I expected going in:

Cropping before scoring

Comparing full channels directly is misleading for two reasons: the scans have dark/torn borders that carry no real signal, and np.roll's wraparound means a shifted image reintroduces garbage pixels at the edge it wrapped from. I score only the interior of each channel — trimming a margin off all four sides before computing the match score — so neither effect pollutes the result.

Going multi-scale

Why brute force stops working, and the fix

The JPEGs are small enough that a real misalignment sits comfortably inside ±15 pixels. The full-resolution .tif scans are 10–20× larger in each dimension, and the true shift grows with the image — often past 100 pixels. Two things get worse at once: the window has to widen to still contain the answer, and each individual comparison costs more because the image itself is bigger. Naively scaling the same approach up would mean checking tens of thousands of shift candidates on a multi-megapixel array, per channel — far too slow.

The fix is an image pyramid: shrink the problem before solving it, then refine.

  1. Downscale both channels by 2× (with anti-aliasing, via skimage.transform.rescale) repeatedly until the image's largest dimension drops below a threshold (I used 400px).
  2. At that coarsest level, run the same brute-force ±15 search as the single-scale version, starting from (0, 0) — the image is now small enough that this is cheap.
  3. Climb back up one level. Double the coarse offset (since the image just doubled in size, so does the true shift, roughly) and use it as the new search center — not zero.
  4. Search a much smaller window (±10) around that center, since the estimate is already close and only needs a small correction.
  5. Repeat until back at full resolution.

The key realization: once you have a coarse estimate, a wide search at a finer level isn't just wasteful — it's actively risky. It can wander toward a different, coincidentally-good-looking region of the image and discard a perfectly good coarse estimate in favor of a false match. Each level after the first should only be correcting the previous level's small residual error, not searching from scratch.

def align(x, y, center=(0, 0), window=15):
    if max(x.shape) < 400:                 # base case
        cy, cx = center
    else:
        x_small = rescale(x, 0.5)
        y_small = rescale(y, 0.5)
        _, (cy0, cx0) = align(x_small, y_small)
        cy, cx = cy0 * 2, cx0 * 2           # scale up
        window = 10                          # narrow refinement

    best = (cy, cx)
    best_score = 0
    for i in range(cy - window, cy + window):
        for j in range(cx - window, cx + window):

Scoring a match: L2 vs. NCC

Euclidean distance (L2)

The simplest metric: sum the squared pixel-wise differences and take the square root. Lower means more similar. This works well as long as the two channels have roughly the same brightness at corresponding points — which is usually, but not always, true.

L2(A, B) = sqrt( sum( (A - B)**2 ) )

Normalized cross-correlation (NCC)

L2 struggles when a channel is systematically brighter or darker than the one it's being matched against — a real possibility since R, G, and B filters don't necessarily transmit the same amount of light. NCC handles this by mean-subtracting and normalizing each patch before comparing, which makes the score invariant to a constant brightness or contrast offset. Higher means more similar.

def ncc(a, b):
    a = (a - a.mean()) / np.linalg.norm(a - a.mean())
    b = (b - b.mean()) / np.linalg.norm(b - b.mean())
    return np.sum(a * b)

I used NCC throughout the pyramid, at every level, once I saw it noticeably outperform raw L2 on images with strong exposure differences between channels.

Where it struggled

emir.tif — the one image the spec allows to fail

Out of the 14 provided images, emir.tif is the single clear failure. NCC on raw pixel values gives a green offset of roughly (24, 49) and a red offset of roughly (−205, 141) — and that red number is the tell. Every other image in the set has a sideways (column) offset under 40 pixels; this one jumps to 141, with a large negative row shift on top of it. The rendered result shows exactly what a bad offset like that produces: a visible second face ghosted behind the Emir's, and heavy red/cyan fringing across the patterned robe.

Why NCC fails here specifically

NCC assumes the three channels are, up to an overall brightness shift, showing the same picture. That assumption breaks down on this plate. The Emir is wearing a bright blue robe: blue cloth reflects strongly through a blue filter and weakly through a red one, so the robe reads as nearly white in the blue channel and nearly black in the red channel. Parts of the background invert the same way in the opposite direction. When the search slides the red channel around looking for the position that best matches blue's brightness pattern, the correct position scores poorly — a bright region is sitting on top of a dark one there — so the search settles on a different, incorrect position instead, and a coarse-level mistake this large is well outside what a ±10 refinement window can recover from.

Mean-subtraction, which is the part of NCC that's supposed to make it brightness-robust, doesn't help here: it only corrects for the whole channel being uniformly brighter or dimmer. This plate's problem is regional, not uniform — different parts of the image flip brightness in different directions relative to each other, so no single global adjustment can fix it.

The standard fix is to align on edge/gradient maps instead of raw pixel intensity, since an edge — the boundary between the robe and the doorway, say — sits in roughly the same place in every channel even when the brightness on either side of it doesn't match. That's listed under "Better features" in the assignment's Bells & Whistles section, which is optional for CS180 (mandatory for CS280A), so I've left emir.tif as my one documented failure rather than implementing it.

A smaller case: icon.tif

Separately, icon.tif is aligned correctly overall, but its green-channel offset is one pixel off from what other implementations report — (40, 17) versus (41, 17). Printing the NCC score for every candidate at the final pyramid level shows why: the scores at i = 40 and i = 41 differ by about 0.00002, an essentially indistinguishable tie rather than a clear winner. Icon's interior is dense with repeating ornamentation (columns, moldings, candle rows), which is exactly the kind of content where a pixel-intensity metric can find two shifts that are both "good enough," since the pattern lines up decently at more than one offset. The red channel on the same image found a sharp, unambiguous peak and landed exactly on the expected value, so this is a one-pixel edge case on one axis, not a failure of the pipeline.
